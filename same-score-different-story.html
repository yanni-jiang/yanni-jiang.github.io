Same Score, Different Story

Is a “Like” the Same Lever as Money?

If social recognition and monetary incentives produce similar behavioral outcomes, should we treat them as interchangeable? I investigated this question through a controlled laboratory comparison of how adults anticipate social and monetary rewards.

The short version

The averages matched. The story didn't end there.

01 / SURFACE

Similar overall outcomes

Social and monetary reward anticipation produced comparable behavioral benefits and largely overlapping average neural responses.

02 / UNDERNEATH

Distinguishable neural patterns

Multivariate analysis revealed reward-related pattern differences that average activation measures did not capture.

03 / PEOPLE

Individual variation

Need to Belong was associated with relative neural response to social reward, although the association did not appear in measured behavior.

Important distinction: This was a controlled academic study, not an A/B test in a live product. Product implications below are hypotheses to validate, not established product outcomes.

01 / The problem

Why question a “common currency”?

A prevailing view in cognitive neuroscience proposes that social approval and non-social rewards such as money rely on a largely shared neural reward system. Much of the supporting evidence comes from similar average activation in the same regions.

There is a parallel temptation in product research: if different incentives lift a top-line metric by a similar amount, it can seem reasonable to treat them as interchangeable. But matching averages alone cannot establish an identical underlying response.

01
Indirect comparisons

Past findings often compared separate participant samples, tasks, or reward formats rather than both incentives within the same individuals.

02
One aggregate measure

Average activation can obscure differences in the distribution or pattern of underlying responses.

03
Differences between people

People do not value social recognition in the same way; measuring individual motivations may reveal associations missed by group averages.

02 / Ownership

My role and collaboration

I was the lead analyst and first author on a six-person team. The principal investigator established the broader research framework and core experiment; I focused on execution, analytical decisions, synthesis, and communication.

What I owned

Participant recruitment and study execution (500+ hours); quality-control and exclusion decisions; end-to-end analysis; integrating findings across methods; visualization; journal writing and research presentations.

What was already defined

Initial research concept, core experimental design, funding, and target sample size were established within the larger NSF-funded project.

How I addressed an analysis capability gap

When an interaction required an analysis method our lab had not used, I initiated collaboration with a lab experienced in multivariate classification. Their team suggested a four-way approach instead of indirectly contrasting two separate two-way classifications. I implemented the method, piloted validation choices, and used structured check-ins to align three senior researchers on recommendations and tradeoffs.

03 / Research approach

A controlled, head-to-head comparison

I organized the analysis around three research questions, from surface-level results to individual differences:

01

Surface. Do social and monetary rewards produce similar observable behavior and average neural responses?

02

Underneath. Can more fine-grained neural patterns reveal reward-related differences that summary measures miss?

03

People. Does Need to Belong relate to an individual's relative response to social versus monetary reward?

The same 107 adults aged 30–45 experienced both reward types. On each trial, a cue indicated the available reward, and participants responded quickly to a visual target. Matched cues, separate no-reward baselines, and adaptive difficulty helped constrain alternative explanations.

Why not rely on interviews alone? Reward anticipation occurred within roughly 2.5 seconds. Behavioral and neural measures captured momentary responses, while a validated survey measured a relevant motivational trait.

Data quality: 120 participants were recruited; 13 were excluded according to predefined criteria for excessive motion, technical issues, or completing less than 75% of the task. Final analytic sample: 107.

04 / Findings

Three findings, three levels of resolution

Finding 01 / Surface

Behavioral benefits looked similar for social and monetary rewards.

Across reward types, anticipating reward improved performance: participants hit the target on approximately 50% vs. 45% of trials and responded in approximately 422 vs. 432 ms (reward vs. no-reward anticipation; both p < .001). The reward-related boost did not differ significantly by reward type.

Average activation engaged substantially overlapping reward-related regions; no significant social-specific reward effect emerged at the average-activation level, and measured connectivity did not significantly differ.

Interpretation: With only top-line results, the incentives looked largely alike. This is not proof that they are equivalent.

Finding 02 / Underneath

More fine-grained neural patterns revealed differences.

A whole-brain multivariate analysis evaluated local activity patterns across four conditions: social reward, social no-reward, monetary reward, and monetary no-reward. Models were evaluated on a participant left out of training.

In several regions, the extent to which the pattern distinguished reward from no reward differed between social and monetary conditions, including where average activation had looked similar (corrected p < .05).

Interpretation: Overlapping mean activity did not imply identical neural representations. A more detailed measure captured information that averages did not.

Finding 03 / People

Social reward response varied with Need to Belong.

Participants with higher Need to Belong scores showed a stronger social-relative-to-monetary neural response in a region relevant to both reward and social cognition (corrected p < .05). Need to Belong was not significantly associated with hit rate or reaction time (all ps > .05).

Interpretation: This correlational association identifies a possible dimension for future investigation, not a validated user segment or a behavioral prediction.

A small behavioral result I chose not to overinterpret

Participants responded about 4 ms faster when social feedback was possible (425 vs. 429 ms, p = .03). The effect was small and did not significantly depend on whether reward was at stake, so I did not frame it as evidence that social reward was more motivating.

05 / Synthesis

Different measurements answered different questions.

At first, the results seemed inconsistent: averages suggested overlap, while activity patterns indicated differences. I understood this as a question of measurement resolution, not a contradiction.

Two user journeys can end at the same conversion rate while involving very different paths. Similarly, two reward conditions can produce similar mean responses while containing distinguishable patterns. But a neural difference does not establish that the rewards have different real-world product outcomes.

What I did not claim: “No significant difference” does not establish equivalence. “Different neural representation” does not mean one incentive is better. This study does not demonstrate that belonging is a ready-to-use targeting variable.

06 / Product thinking

Product hypotheses worth testing

These are possible research directions informed by a controlled lab experiment. None should drive targeting or a roadmap without validation using real product data.

01

Look beyond matching top-line results.

If social recognition and reward points yield comparable engagement, compare more granular behavioral evidence—such as retention curves or usage paths—and consider qualitative follow-up before treating them as interchangeable.

Evidence: Findings 1–2 · Confidence: Medium; the product analogy is not itself directly tested.

02

Pair behavioral metrics with a motivational measure.

A short attitudinal survey may help investigate differences that click-through, speed, or conversion metrics do not show. Validate whether this adds predictive value in the product setting.

Evidence: Finding 3 · Confidence: Medium; based on one study's individual-difference association.

03

Explore belonging, but do not personalize yet.

Test whether the effect of social incentives on meaningful product outcomes varies with belonging-related motivations. Treat belonging as a candidate research variable rather than an established segment.

Evidence: Finding 3 · Confidence: Medium–low; correlational and not yet behaviorally validated.

07 / Contribution and boundaries

Impact, with limits made clear

The main outcome was a first-author publication in the Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience (2026) and a more nuanced understanding of reward processing. I shared the research through talks, showcases, and peer-reviewed writing. This work did not produce a shipped product feature.

For product research, the transferable skills are controlled experimentation, combining behavioral and attitudinal measures, quality-control judgment, reconciling complex evidence, and explaining uncertainty to different audiences.

What the study could not tell us

  • Which reward is inherently stronger: social and monetary incentives were not calibrated to the same subjective-value scale.
  • Whether belonging defines an actionable user segment: the association was correlational and observed in a single study.
  • How broadly results generalize: participants were adults aged 30–45 (78.5% women), with a sample size constrained by funding.
  • Whether results translate directly to products: a stranger's approval in a brief video is not equivalent to feedback from friends, colleagues, or communities.
08 / Next steps

What I would investigate next

  1. Run an in-product incentive comparison with a short Need to Belong measure and a prespecified behavioral outcome such as retention.
  2. Interview users with different belonging motivations to understand when recognition feels valuable and authentic.
  3. Use more realistic social feedback and perceived-value ratings to improve ecological validity and subjective-value matching.
  4. Study links to everyday social behavior with appropriate consent, to test whether neural response differences have external behavioral relevance.
What I learned

The measure we choose shapes what we are able to see.

“No difference detected” is not the same as “no difference exists.” Sometimes a flat result reflects what the measure cannot see—not the absence of a meaningful distinction.
