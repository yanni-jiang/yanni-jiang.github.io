01 / The challenge

Can similar averages hide meaningful differences?

A widely held neuroscience account proposes that social and monetary rewards draw on shared reward-related systems. But similar average activation does not, by itself, establish identical underlying processing.

In product terms, it is like seeing similar top-line engagement from recognition and reward points and assuming they are interchangeable. I wanted to test what might be missed by that conclusion.

01

Indirect comparisons

Earlier studies often involved different people, tasks, and incentives.

02

Aggregate measures

Averages may hide distinctions in patterns of response.

03

Different motivations

People vary in how much they value social approval.

02 / Ownership

What I owned — and how I worked with others.

I was the lead analyst and first author on a six-person team. The principal investigator established the larger research framework and core design; I owned the study execution, analytical decisions, synthesis, and dissemination.

My contribution

Led recruitment and more than 500 hours of study execution; applied data-quality criteria; built the analysis pipeline; reconciled findings across methods; visualized results; and communicated them through a journal article, talks, and research showcases.

Team context

Initial concept, core experiment, funding, and target sample size belonged to the larger NSF-funded project.

The methodological decision I helped navigate

When the team needed an unfamiliar multivariate approach, I initiated a collaboration with a lab that had relevant expertise. Their researchers recommended a four-way classification rather than indirectly comparing two separate two-way models. I implemented and piloted the approach, evaluated validation choices, and brought evidence and tradeoffs to structured check-ins with senior collaborators.

03 / Research design

Same people. Both rewards. Multiple measures.

All 107 adults experienced both social and monetary reward conditions, letting us compare them within the same participants.

STEP 01
See a cue

A visual cue indicates the possible social or monetary reward.

→
STEP 02
Respond

Participants respond quickly to a visual target.

→
STEP 03
Receive feedback

Social approval or a wallet/coins animation indicates the result.

Each reward type had a separate no-reward baseline, cues were matched, and adaptive difficulty kept success near half of trials. The measures were chosen to capture different levels of evidence:

Behavior · hit rate & reaction time Neuroimaging · average activation & patterns Survey · Need to Belong

Reward anticipation unfolded over roughly 2.5 seconds — a moment that retrospective interviews alone would not capture reliably.

Experiment conditions and data-quality details

Social feedback: a smiling face with a thumbs-up for reward, or a neutral expression for no reward. Monetary feedback: coins dropping into a wallet for reward, or a closing wallet with no coins. Each reward category had its own neutral baseline video.

Quality control: 120 people were recruited; 13 were excluded under predefined criteria for movement, technical issues, or completing fewer than 75% of trials. Final analytic sample: 107.

04 / Key findings

Three findings. Three levels of resolution.

Instead of relying on one outcome, I examined what was visible at the aggregate level, what emerged from finer-grained patterns, and what varied across participants.

Finding 01 — At the surface

Reward helped performance; the benefit looked similar across reward types.

When any reward was at stake, people were faster and more accurate than in no-reward anticipation. The reward-related behavioral boost did not significantly differ between social and monetary conditions; average neural responses also showed substantial overlap.

Finding 02 — Underneath

More detailed neural patterns distinguished the reward conditions.

A whole-brain multivariate analysis examined four conditions (social reward/no reward and monetary reward/no reward), testing on participants excluded from model training. In several regions, reward-vs-no-reward pattern separation differed between social and monetary conditions, including regions with similar mean activation (corrected p < .05).

Finding 03 — Differences between people

Need to Belong related to neural sensitivity — not measured behavior.

Higher Need to Belong scores were associated with stronger social-relative-to-monetary neural responses in a reward/social-cognition region (corrected p < .05). No significant association emerged for hit rate or reaction time (all ps > .05).

What this means: Belonging is worth testing as an individual-difference factor, not using as a validated product segment.
An additional small effect I chose not to overinterpret

Participants were around 4 ms faster when social feedback was possible (425 vs. 429 ms, p = .03). This effect did not significantly depend on whether a reward was at stake, so I did not interpret it as evidence that social reward was more motivating.

05 / Synthesis & product thinking

A useful lesson about what metrics miss.

Similar outcomes don't always imply identical underlying responses.

The apparent conflict between average and multivariate results was a difference in measurement resolution, not evidence that one reward was necessarily more effective. The product parallels below are hypotheses to test, not results from an in-product experiment.

01

Investigate beyond the headline metric

When two incentives produce similar engagement, compare usage paths, retention curves, and other granular behavioral evidence before deciding they are interchangeable.

Basis: Findings 1–2 · Confidence: medium, product analogy untested
02

Combine behavioral and attitudinal evidence

Test whether a short motivation measure adds information that clicks or conversion alone miss.

Basis: Finding 3 · Confidence: medium, single-study association
03

Validate belonging before segmentation

Examine whether social incentives affect real-world outcomes differently across users; do not personalize based on the lab finding alone.

Basis: Finding 3 · Confidence: medium–low, correlational

Important boundary: No significant difference is not proof of equivalence; neural distinctions do not establish different product outcomes. This was an academic laboratory study, not an A/B test.

06 / Contribution & next questions

A publication, a research contribution, and questions to carry forward.

↗
First-author publication · Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience (2026)Shared across research presentations and peer-reviewed publication; no shipped product feature is claimed.

For product research, the skills I would carry forward include controlled comparison, combining attitudinal and behavioral measures, protecting data quality, evaluating tradeoffs, and communicating uncertainty clearly.

What I would study next

  1. Run a real product experiment comparing social recognition and reward points, with an a priori behavioral outcome such as retention and a brief belonging measure.
  2. Interview users to understand when recognition feels meaningful or authentic, and test more ecologically valid social rewards.
  3. Measure perceived reward value and examine whether neural differences relate to everyday social behavior, with appropriate consent.
Limitations and boundaries of the evidence

Reward matching: Social and monetary incentives were not calibrated to equal subjective value, so the study cannot determine which is inherently more motivating.

Segmentation: Need to Belong was a correlational finding from one study, not a validated targeting rule.

Sample: Adults aged 30–45, 78.5% women; findings cannot be assumed to generalize to all populations.

Ecological validity: A stranger's approval in a brief video is a simplified proxy for social recognition in actual products.

What this taught me

The measure we choose shapes what we are able to see.

“No difference detected” is not the same as “no difference exists.” Sometimes the limitation is the metric, not the experience.
