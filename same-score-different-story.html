01 / The question & context

The question

Product teams often use both social recognition and monetary incentives to motivate behavior. When both rewards produce similar top-line results, should teams treat them as interchangeable? I investigated a controlled laboratory version of this question by comparing how 107 adults anticipated social and monetary rewards.

Picture an app where friend kudos and reward points both lift engagement by the same amount. The averages match, but are people being motivated in the same way? This project looked beneath the average to find out.

Why the “common currency” view deserves a closer look

A prevailing idea in cognitive neuroscience is the “common currency” view: social rewards (such as approval) and non-social rewards (such as money) are processed by a largely shared neural system. Support for this view comes mostly from studies that find similar average activation in the same brain regions for both kinds of reward.

Product teams often reason in a parallel way. When two incentives produce a similar lift in a top-line metric, it is tempting to conclude that they work through the same underlying mechanism, and that either one will do.

However, similar overall responses do not by themselves show a shared system, and the evidence behind this inference had three gaps:

01
Indirect comparisons. Many conclusions were drawn across separate studies using different participants, tasks, and reward types. That is closer to comparing two product experiments run on different user populations than testing the alternatives head-to-head.
02
One aggregate metric. Most studies relied on a single summary measure. Similar averages can still hide meaningful differences in the patterns producing them.
03
User heterogeneity. People differ in how much social approval motivates them compared with a tangible payoff. Averaging across people can make two rewards look alike even when they motivate different people in different ways, and few studies measured how much people actually care about social reward.

Why it matters. If two incentives look equivalent at the top line but work differently underneath, treating them as interchangeable could lead to poor product decisions, such as removing a social feature that matters to some users, or reading a flat A/B test as evidence that the underlying experience is the same.

The decision and research questions

The decision this project informs: when social and monetary incentives produce similar top-line results, should teams treat them as interchangeable, or test them separately, including for different types of users?

I broke this decision into three questions, moving from aggregate outcomes to individual differences:

  1. Surface: Do social and monetary rewards produce the same observable behavior and similar average neural responses?
  2. Underneath: If the two rewards look similar at the surface, can a more fine-grained measure reveal differences that the top-line metric misses?
  3. Segment: Do individual differences, specifically how strongly someone values social belonging, relate to how they respond to each reward?

Each question required a different kind of evidence, which shaped the study design.

This is the habit I would bring to product teams: come with options and evidence, and keep stakeholders aligned in minutes, not hours.

03 / The approach

Approach: a controlled, head-to-head comparison

The design addressed the three gaps directly: the same participants, a controlled comparison, and multiple measures instead of one.

Same people, both rewards. All 107 adults aged 30–45 experienced both social and monetary rewards, so differences could not come from comparing different groups. Mid-career adults are also less studied in this literature and may be closer to the working-age users many digital products serve.

A controlled comparison. On each trial, a simple visual cue showed which type of reward (social or monetary) was available, and participants had a brief window to respond to a target on the screen.

As in any well-controlled experiment, everything else was held constant. Cues were matched across conditions, each reward had its own baseline, and task difficulty adapted so participants succeeded about half the time. This helped isolate the effect of reward type from visual or performance differences.

Why not interviews? The key moment, anticipating a possible reward, lasted only about 2.5 seconds, which is hard to capture through retrospective self-report alone. Pairing objective behavioral and neural measures with a validated survey let me compare what participants said they valued with how they responded in the moment.

Protecting data quality. I applied the study’s predefined quality-control criteria, excluding 13 of 120 recruited participants because of excessive movement, technical failure, or completing less than 75% of the task. The final analytic sample included 107 participants.

04 / What I found

What I found

Three findings, moving from the surface to the individual.

FINDING 01 / SURFACE

1. On the surface, a “like” and money produced comparable results

What we saw. When a reward was at stake, participants hit more targets (about 50% vs. 45% of trials) and responded faster (about 422 ms vs. 432 ms), both p < .001. The reward boost did not differ significantly between social and monetary rewards.

At the level of average brain activity, social and monetary reward anticipation showed substantial overlap in regions associated with reward processing. Although some regions responded differently to social versus monetary information, we did not find a social-specific reward effect in the regions of primary interest, or significant differences in connectivity among the key reward-related regions examined.

What it means. Judged by top-line results alone, the two incentives would look largely equivalent, and a conventional evaluation might reasonably have treated them as interchangeable.

FINDING 02 / PATTERNS

2. A more fine-grained measure could tell them apart

What we saw. A machine-learning analysis searched the whole brain for local activity patterns that could separate four conditions (social reward, social no-reward, monetary reward, monetary no-reward), always testing on a participant the model had not seen during training. In several regions, how well the pattern separated reward from no reward differed between social and monetary rewards. This included regions where average activation had looked similar for both rewards (corrected p < .05).

What it means. Matching averages did not guarantee identical underlying responses. In this study, two incentives with similar top-line outcomes still left distinguishable patterns underneath.

FINDING 03 / PEOPLE

3. Social rewards mattered more to some people, and behavior didn’t show it

What we saw. Participants with a higher Need to Belong score showed a stronger response to social reward (relative to monetary reward) in a region where reward processing and social cognition intersect (corrected p < .05). Need to Belong did not significantly relate to hit rate or reaction time (all ps > .05).

What it means. This is a correlation from a single study, so it does not show that Need to Belong defines a user segment. It does suggest that belonging is worth examining when thinking about segmentation, and that behavioral data alone may not reveal this kind of difference. Pairing behavioral data with attitudinal measures is one way to look for it.

Additional result I chose not to build on

A result I chose not to build on. Participants were about 4 ms faster when social feedback was possible (425 ms vs. 429 ms, p = .03). The effect was small and did not significantly depend on whether a reward was at stake, so it looks like an effect of cue type rather than a reward-specific advantage. I reported it, but did not treat it as evidence for a meaningful reward difference.

05 / Interpretation & product thinking

Reconciling “conflicting” signals

At first glance, the results seemed inconsistent: coarse measures suggested the rewards were similar, while more fine-grained measures showed differences. I treated that not as a contradiction, but as a question of measurement resolution.

Aggregate measures compress information. Two conditions can produce the same average while differing in the pattern underneath, much as two user journeys can end at the same conversion rate through different paths. Viewed at different levels of resolution, the findings were not contradictory; they were answering different questions.

The conclusion: in this study, social and monetary rewards showed substantial overlap at the aggregate level, but they were distinguishable in how they were represented, and the neural difference between them was more pronounced for people with a higher need to belong, though this did not show up in behavior.

What I didn’t claim. “No difference detected” is not evidence that two rewards are identical, and “different” does not mean “better.” The study shows that social and monetary rewards can produce similar top-line outcomes while differing in their underlying response patterns. It does not show that one reward is more motivating than the other, or that Need to Belong defines a user segment.

Product Hypotheses to Validate

The following hypotheses are drawn from a controlled lab study and should be treated as directions for further testing, rather than product prescriptions. Each is tied to a finding, and the confidence rating reflects how directly the study supports it.

PriorityHypothesisBasisConfidence
1
Similar top-line results may not mean interchangeable incentives. When two incentives look alike on a top-line metric, check whether more granular patterns tell a different story. In a product, “granular” could mean retention curves, usage paths, or short follow-up interviews rather than brain data.
Findings 1–2 · Medium. Supported by the contrast between aggregate and pattern-level results in this study, though pattern-level differences alone do not show different behavioral outcomes
2
Pairing behavioral metrics with a short motivation measure may reveal differences that clicks, speed, or conversion miss. Worth testing when evaluating social features.
Finding 3 · Medium. Based on an individual-difference association in one study
3
Belonging may be a useful dimension to explore when examining how social incentives work for different users. Test whether effects differ across user groups before personalizing, and treat belonging as a hypothesis to examine, not a targeting rule.
Finding 3 · Medium–low. Correlational finding that needs validation in product data

For example (hypothetical): if a fitness app finds that friend kudos and reward points both increase weekly workouts by 8%, the next step is not to conclude that the choice of incentive does not matter. It is to ask whether the two incentives work differently underneath, or work better for different users.

Each hypothesis should be validated with real product data before it influences targeting or roadmap decisions.

Impact

As academic research, the impact of this work is primarily on how social and monetary rewards are measured and interpreted, rather than on shipped product features.

Contribution

↗

↗

What transfers to product research. Head-to-head experimental design, combining behavioral and attitudinal measures, careful data-quality decisions, calibrating confidence in conclusions, and translating findings for different audiences.

Limitations: what I could not conclude
  • Which reward is stronger. The social and monetary rewards were not calibrated onto a common subjective-value scale, so the study cannot determine which was inherently more motivating.
  • Correlation, not segmentation. The Need to Belong association was correlational and observed in a single study, so it is a hypothesis for future segmentation work rather than a validated segment or targeting rule.
  • Who it applies to. Healthy adults aged 30–45, 78.5% women, with sample size set by funding. The findings should not be assumed to generalize to clinical populations or substantially different age groups.
  • Ecological validity. A brief video of a stranger’s approval is a simplified proxy for social rewards from friends, colleagues, or communities in real products.

What I’d do next

The next step is to move from controlled lab findings toward real product behavior.

  1. Test the belonging hypothesis in-product. Run a social-versus-points incentive experiment, pair it with a short Need to Belong measure, and test whether reward effects on retention differ across users.
  2. Add qualitative research to understand why. Interview users with higher and lower Need to Belong scores about when recognition feels meaningful, when it does not, and what makes a social reward feel authentic.
  3. Increase ecological validity. Test feedback from people participants actually know, and collect perceived-value ratings so social and monetary rewards can be compared on a more equivalent subjective-value scale.
  4. Link neural sensitivity to real-world social behavior. Test whether neural responses to social versus monetary reward are associated with naturally occurring social behavior (for example, how people communicate in social settings), using data collected with appropriate consent. This would show whether the lab differences map onto how people actually behave in social environments.

What this taught me: the measure we choose shapes what we are able to see. “No difference detected” is not the same as “no difference exists”; sometimes the limitation is the metric, not the experience.
