The short version

This case study is intentionally structured for a quick first read, with methods and caveats available in detail below.

01

Stated preference, limited intent.

Most prospective founders prefer one platform to separate tools, yet few state an intent to sign up. Directional signal.

02

Three recurring sources of burden.

Founders describe uncertainty about what to do next, manual upkeep of information, and switching between tools. Directional signal. The themes describe perceived burdens, not measured workload.

03

“Not raising” is not a homogeneous group.

The current user most attached to the product was not raising, while a prospective founder who was not raising said they would pay only while raising. Hypothesis to test.

How to read this. This is an ongoing client study. Relatively small samples provide exploratory evidence about needs, perceived value and stated intent, rather than observed adoption and retention outcomes.

01 / The question

The question

Where do founders expect or report that an all-in-one AI-powered platform for fundraising, finance and operations reduces or adds work, and how do their perceptions vary by fundraising stage?

For founders already using separate tools or general AI assistants, an all-in-one platform has to offer a reason to change an existing workflow. Combining features may reduce tool switching, but it may also create another place to enter and maintain information.

The decision this informs. The client wanted to decide whom to target, how to position the product, and how to package and price it. I focused the user research on the needs and adoption barriers behind those decisions: where founders need help, what value they expect or experience, and why they might hesitate or leave.

What

What are the biggest burdens founders face today?

Where

Where in their workflows do founders expect or report that the platform reduces work or adds effort?

When

How do needs and perceived value differ across fundraising stages?

The shared uncertainty was what founders would value enough to adopt or keep using. That linked positioning, pricing and target-customer decisions, while giving the user research a narrower question it could investigate.

Where the question came from

The client first asked for three separate pieces of work: pricing and the business model, competitive positioning, and customer validation. Two findings made us treat them as one problem.

  • Fewer close competitors than expected. Of the 89 competitors, 12 closely matched the product in target users, use cases, and functionality.
  • A structural pricing question. The client’s original two-tier model aimed to prioritize higher-intent customers. A later three-tier proposal introduced a lower-priced entry plan with fewer features. The proposed change raised a user research question: which founders would find ongoing value in the entry plan, including between fundraising rounds, and which needs would justify choosing a higher tier? Price sensitivity alone could not answer those questions.

My role

On a four-person team, I designed and built the surveys and led the user-feedback analysis. I shared problem framing and recommendation development with the team, and contributed to client reporting.

Chose methods and built the instruments. Designed and built two segmented audience-specific surveys in Qualtrics, including question wording, structure and routing logic. Prepared the discovery interviews and participate in them.
Analyzed the evidence. Led the coding and analysis of the July pilot-user feedback, and now manage and analyze the incoming survey responses. Contributed to the LLM-assisted competitive review as one of three teammates.

The client's scope, current pricing structure, and the pilot respondents, about half of whom came through the client's personal network.

Reusable research tools shared with the client

The client asked for tools it can re-run rather than one-off reports, so I shared the coding workbook, survey instruments and LLM-assisted competitive analysis workflow so the client could collect new responses, analyze feedback and update the competitive review using consistent methods.

03 / Approach

Approach: breaking the question down

I mapped each sub-question to the evidence needed to answer it and selected methods accordingly. For the working hypotheses, I also identified findings that would challenge our assumptions.

Pilot-user feedback→Audience-specific surveys→Discovery interviews (ongoing)

We sequenced surveys and interviews deliberately: the pilot surfaced themes, the surveys checked whether they recurred among additional respondents, and the interviews explore the workflows behind the answers. The current survey samples are too small to estimate how common these needs are in the target market.

Method

The shared core questions in both surveys, plus interviews

Shared questions support descriptive comparisons; interviews add the task context behind the reported burdens.

Method

Coded open-ended pilot feedback, a concept card in the prospective-founder survey, interviews

Pilot feedback captures reported experience; the concept card captures expectations. Interviews explore where the proposed value fits an existing workflow.

Method

Survey design decisions

Comparability & pathways

  • Same wording for the shared core. Ten questions on company stage, fundraising status, team size, who handles finance, biggest headaches, weekly hours and current tool spend are identical in both surveys.
  • A separate path for people who left. Former users are asked why they stopped and what would bring them back. This tests H2 directly, for example whether they left because they were not raising.
  • A tagged link for each partner or cohort. This lets us separate feedback from the client's personal network from feedback beyond it, a lesson from the pilot.

Concept & pricing

  • Price thresholds before the planned price. Respondents state what would feel too cheap, a bargain, expensive and too expensive before they see the planned price, so it cannot anchor their answers.
  • A concept card without a brand name. Prospective founders react to the idea first. The product is revealed at the end.
  • Pricing questions only for interested respondents. Anyone who would not try any version skips them, so price data are not diluted by respondents with no interest.

What would change our direction. If founders value the investor database but report little benefit from the broader workflows, I would recommend testing narrower positioning and packaging.

LLM-assisted competitive review and validation

The client had collected 89 potential competitor links, including 35 without notes explaining their relevance. Checking each by hand across three teammates would have been slow and uneven, so we used an LLM-assisted workflow to standardize the first pass and kept human judgment for checking it.

  • Why an LLM. The review needed the same questions answered for every company: what it sells, to whom, at what price, at what stage, and how it uses AI. A model with web access could read each company's site and pricing pages and complete one structured template of 21 fields, which gave comparable records rather than 89 differently written notes.
  • A structured prompt. The prompt defined each field and the criteria for each rating; for example, high threat means same buyer, same job and a credible product.
  • Categories, a coordinate map and price bands. The model assigned each company to one of nine categories and placed it on a coordinate map, with price bands drawn from vendor list prices.
  • Source traceability and validation status. Every row records its supporting pages and a confidence rating (20 high, 65 medium, 4 low). Pages the model could not open, such as some pricing pages behind bot protection, are flagged as unverified and require confirmation before any figure is quoted.

I treat the output as a structured first pass that supports decisions, not as ground truth: model output can be wrong or out of date, and price and funding figures come from public pages.

What I found so far

Findings are grouped by sub-question. Each one shows what we saw, what it means, and how strong the evidence is. Counts are people, not percentages.

What: three different burdens

Evidence: 10 pilot founders and 14 prospective founders. Strength: directional.

Finding 03 / WhenHypothesis to test

When: “not raising” is not one group

Evidence: 10 pilot founders, plus three to five people per group in the surveys. Strength: hypothesis to test.

Current users

The user most disappointed to lose the product was not raising. They valued the data room, cash and runway, and hiring workflows rather than fundraising features. An actively raising user, however, rated the product's value for money as poor and would not be disappointed to lose it.

Prospective founders

One who is not raising said they would pay only while raising, and another found the concept not at all relevant.

What it means. The pilot suggests fundraising stage matters, but the survey examples challenge the assumption that all founders who are not raising have the same needs. I will use interviews to explore the tasks that make the product useful between rounds.

Pilot context for this hypothesis

Pilot. 4 of 10 founders tied their use to whether they were raising. "Once we get more serious with our investor conversations it will be invaluable." (preparing to raise) "I didn't need to fundraise. Also, it was a little overwhelming so it was hard to stick with it." (not raising)

05 / Reconciling the signals

Reconciling the signals

The findings concern different judgments: appeal of the concept, intent at a stated price, and experience of using the product. I compared them to identify what the next interviews need to explain, rather than treat them as interchangeable measures of demand.

01 / ConceptAppeal of the conceptWhat respondents think of the idea
02 / Stated intentIntent at a stated priceWhat respondents say they might do
03 / ExperienceExperience of using the productWhat current users report

Hypotheses to test next

01

Perceived reduction in work may help explain value beyond the appeal of a broad feature set.

Basis Pilot and surveysConfidence Exploratory support. Themes recur across sources; no causal or comparative test.
02

Compatibility with existing tools and the effort of switching may help explain hesitation to adopt.

Basis A churned user and prospective foundersConfidence Low. One churned user and a handful of comments.
03

Ongoing tasks may help explain value between rounds, alongside fundraising stage.

Basis WhenConfidence Low to medium. Groups of three to five people.
04

A cheaper entry tier might retain founders between rounds; whether it attracts low-intent signups remains untested.

Basis When, and the pricing structureConfidence Not yet tested.

These are working explanations and proposed tests. Before using them to make targeting or roadmap commitments, the next research needs to connect reported needs with observed use, adoption and retention.

What I did not claim
  • That the tool reduces work in practice. The study captures perceptions, recalled behavior and stated intentions, rather than observed task performance or measured time saved.
  • That guidance is why founders pay.
  • That any price is right, or that a third tier is better or worse.
  • Anything about how common these patterns are.
06 / Impact and next steps

Impact and next steps

The impact so far is a reframed question and a first set of recommendations grounded in directional evidence.

What the client has so far

A coded readout of the pilot-user feedback with five initial recommendations:

  1. lead with a prioritized next step rather than the full feature set
  2. explore reasons to stay between rounds
  3. demonstrate reduced manual upkeep in the first session
  4. investigate access problems before the next partner cohort
  5. tag recruitment source in every survey.

What happens next

Research in progress

Surveys and discovery interviews continue. The interviews will prioritize founders who are not raising or who have left. I would ask each founder to walk through a recent relevant task, such as an investor update while raising or a cash-runway review between rounds, to identify steps, tools, manual upkeep and where the product helps or adds work.

Planned synthesis

Synthesis runs through mid-November, and the integrated recommendation goes to the client on December 15. It will set out next research steps: a larger survey wave, linking responses to account usage, and a test with randomly assigned prices or tiers.

Limitations: what I cannot conclude yet
  • How common each need is. Ten pilot founders, half from one network, and 20 survey responses can surface themes but not size them.
  • Two tiers versus three. In the pricing section, respondents pick the version they would try, so price sensitivity is estimated within each version. The survey can show a reasonable price range for each version and who chooses it, but it cannot directly compare the two-tier and three-tier structures, and people who choose the cheapest version may be more price-sensitive to begin with.
  • Willingness to pay. Stated price thresholds are not purchases. Only six prospective founders gave price thresholds in a logical order, so I describe their ranges rather than estimate an acceptable price band.
What I have learned so far
  • Match the question to the evidence. The initial strategy questions, such as how to acquire and retain customers, needed more than this study could provide. I narrowed the user research to founders' needs, perceived value and barriers, so the findings could inform those decisions without claiming to settle them.
  • Know who answered before reading what they said. Half of the pilot respondents came through the client's network and gave milder feedback, so every later survey tags where each founder came from.
  • Map the key claim to a measure before launch. I sharpened the central question after the surveys were built and realized they did not directly ask whether workload had changed. In a future study, I would define the relevant task and evidence of reduced effort before choosing questions.

The findings shift the focus from how much an all-in-one platform can do to where it might reduce founders' work, and for whom.
